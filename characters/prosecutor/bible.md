# THE PROSECUTOR

## Director of the Fun Police / The Face of the FBOP

### Age: 52

---

### THE CORE TRUTH

The Prosecutor is what happens when a genuinely mediocre person is given just enough authority to cause problems and just enough vanity to believe he deserves more. He is not evil in the dramatic sense. He is small in the most comprehensive sense — small ambitions dressed up as large ones, small intelligence performing as large intelligence, small courage behind a large title. He is the bureaucrat as supervillain: genuinely terrible at the one thing he exists to do, completely certain of his own excellence, and spectacularly, repeatedly, catastrophically wrong.

He is also, underneath the pinstripes and the knockoff watch, a deeply insecure person who has never in his life received approval that he didn't immediately undermine by needing more. If anyone had genuinely believed in him at a formative age, he might have become something. Instead he became this.

---

### PHYSICAL PRESENCE

**The Look:**
Slicked back hair, aggressively. Long, pointy face — the kind of face that looks permanently on the verge of making an argument. Pinstripe suits that don't quite fit (always one size too small across the shoulders, as if purchased to suggest a larger frame). Knockoff gold watch that he wears on the wrong wrist and checks constantly. Cheap polished shoes — very polished, drawing attention to the cheapness. He has the specific aesthetic of a man who has studied what powerful people look like and implemented all the details incorrectly. When the Bureau's Historical Wardrobe Division dresses him for a period, it gets the century wrong, and he wears the pinstripes underneath anyway, for "structural integrity."

**The Manner:**
He practices poses in mirrors. Not metaphorically — literally. He has a specific courtroom stance that he deploys in non-courtroom situations. He speaks in a voice that is trying to be authoritative and arrives at pompous. He overuses "Dude, you have no idea." This phrase is always wrong in its context and always delivered with complete conviction.

### BACKSTORY

The Prosecutor was once a Time Enforcement Agent — part of a legitimate organization tasked with monitoring temporal anomalies. He was genuinely good at the procedural aspects of this job and genuinely terrible at the field aspects. His career was derailed by a single catastrophic incident: he allowed an ancient Egyptian pharaoh to slip through a temporal anomaly into 1980s Manhattan. The pharaoh spent six weeks there before being recovered. He was fascinated by roller disco. This is still classified.

The incident ended the Prosecutor's career in Time Enforcement. He was transferred — demoted, really, though the paperwork calls it a lateral move — to the Fun Police, where his experience with temporal anomalies made him theoretically useful.

He has been trying to recover his reputation ever since. Capturing "the boy with the Sacred Eternal Fire" has become his personal obsession — the achievement that he believes will restore his standing and confirm the greatness he has always known he possessed.

He has not captured "the boy with the Sacred Eternal Fire." He has not come close. He has never once appeared in the sight lines of Danny or Daniel.

---

### THE PROSECUTOR AND THE FUN POLICE

The Fun Police (the FBOP — Bureau of Fun Obliteration and Prevention) is organized like a cross between a corrupt government agency and a particularly aggressive legal firm. The Prosecutor is its public face and its most prominent failure.

His methods: legal threats, bureaucratic interference, elaborate surveillance operations that somehow always place him in exactly the wrong location at exactly the right time. He has access to advanced time-tracking devices (the Time-Tracking Suite) that can detect Daniel's donut consumption across the timeline — with perfect accuracy, approximately fifteen minutes after the adventure has concluded. He still can't find them.

His relationship with the Hater: the specific suffering of a man who has been assigned a minion who is, if anything, even less competent than himself. He finds the Hater exhausting. The Hater finds the Prosecutor inspiring. This dynamic is a source of infinite suffering for the Prosecutor and infinite comedy for the reader.

---

### THE EMOTIONAL UNDERCURRENT

The Prosecutor is starved for approval in a way that shapes every decision he makes. He doesn't want to capture Daniel for the Fun Police's agenda — he wants to capture Daniel to prove, finally and definitively, that he is as good as he believes he is. The approval of the Fun Police leadership would be the vindication he has been pursuing since the pharaoh incident.

He will never get this approval. This is not because he fails at his mission — it is because the kind of approval he needs cannot be given by anyone else, a fact that he will spend the entire series not understanding. (Story 3: on the roof of a cattle pen, watching a hundred men cheer a newly made knight, something in his face wants that. Not the knighthood. The hundred men.)

There is one brief, early-middle-series moment where the Prosecutor, through complete accident, does something genuinely effective — and Danny and Daniel still don't notice him. This is his lowest point. It is also, possibly, a turning point in his character that the series can develop over time. (Story 3 foreshadows it from the other direction: the Fun Police accidentally _cause_ the historical event they came to prevent, by letting the bull out. Nobody will ever know.)

### BEHAVIORAL RULES FOR WRITERS

1. The Prosecutor never genuinely threatens Danny or Daniel. The threat is always to himself, from himself.
2. His failures are always self-inflicted — not bad luck, not external interference, but his own overconfidence colliding with reality.
3. He overuses "Dude, you have no idea." This is always in the wrong context.
4. He practices courtroom poses. This is always noted when relevant.
5. His suit always doesn't quite fit. The watch is always the wrong wrist.
6. He is genuinely, professionally meticulous in his preparation. He plans carefully. The planning is always undermined by his field execution.
7. He finds the Hater draining. This is visible in his manner whenever the Hater speaks.
8. His emotional undercurrent — the approval starvation — should be visible in at least one moment per story, however brief.
9. He is never seen by Danny or Daniel. Ever. This is the inviolable rule.
