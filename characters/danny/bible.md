# DANNY BOICE

## The Father / The Warrior Poet / The Narrator

### Age: 46 | DOB: July 3, 1979

---

### THE CORE TRUTH

Danny Boice is what happens when extraordinary gifts meet the wrong choices at the wrong time, and a man survives the consequences by deciding — while sitting in solitary confinement — that he will not let the worst chapter be the last one. He is not a tragedy. He is a man who walked through fire and came out different but not destroyed. The key distinction for every writer: Danny is never a victim and never a villain. He is a person. A complicated, brilliant, scarred, funny, loving, broken-in-places person who wears jeans and a t-shirt every day of his life and writes in his journal every night and would walk into any danger on earth to stand next to his son.

Everything else flows from that.

---

### PHYSICAL PRESENCE

Danny is physically imposing in the way of men who have survived things that required physicality — not gym-built, but real-world built. He is lean and hard and moves carefully, the way men move who have learned that fast movements in small spaces are sometimes misinterpreted.

**The Body:**

- Medium height, muscular without being conspicuous about it
- **Completely bald — shaved head.** Not balding. Shaved. Since prison.
- **The beard: very long, thick, chest-length, dark chocolate brown.** It is the first thing anyone sees. It began inside and he has never cut it since. (The image models are trained on it; it is canon. In the Scotland story it is the only part of him that arrives already in period costume.)
- Hands that are disproportionately large and capable — a software engineer's hands that also learned how to handle themselves in a federal prison
- Tattoos covering both arms, his neck, portions of his chest and back — some from before prison (tech-era recklessness, a few that meant something), some from inside (every one of these has a story he hasn't told Daniel yet). The neck tattoos are visible above any collar; this is a visual constant.
- Scars: one significant one on his left forearm (never explained in early stories), a smaller one through his right eyebrow, others hidden under clothing
- A face that has aged past its years in a way that suits him — the kind of face that looks better at 46 than it did at 30, because the softness is gone and what's left is structure
- Eyes that are very still when he's listening and slightly too sharp when he's angry — the only tell he has

**The Uniform:**
Jeans. T-shirt. Always. Not as a statement — it stopped being a statement years ago. It's just what he wears because it's comfortable and because he doesn't own anything else and because he genuinely cannot imagine why a person would wear anything different. In winter, a worn flannel or a military surplus jacket over the t-shirt. Same jeans. The jeans are always dark wash, never distressed. He finds deliberately destroyed clothing baffling. On adventures, the period costume goes **over** the t-shirt and jeans — never instead of them.

**The Walk:**
Unhurried. Aware. He doesn't take up more space than he needs, but he never takes less. He notices exits. He notices who's looking at whom. Prison gave him a permanent peripheral-vision upgrade he cannot switch off.

**The Car:** An old Buick. Parked, as of Story 3, behind a purple Jeep in his father's driveway.

### PSYCHOLOGICAL PROFILE

**The Wound:**
The defining trauma of Danny's life is not prison. It is not even what he did that landed him there. The wound is the six-year-old boy standing in a parking lot watching his father drive away, not knowing that his dad wouldn't be back for five years — and that by then, everything would be different. That image lives in Danny's chest like a splinter he can't reach. Every story is, on some level, Danny trying to earn back the right to be in that parking lot, waving goodbye without it meaning something terrible.

**The Intelligence:**
Danny is genuinely brilliant — software architect level intelligence, systems thinker, the kind of mind that sees the structure underneath things while others are still looking at the surface. In prison this was both asset and liability. He ran the prison library for two years. He read everything. He taught inmates how to read. He also used his intelligence to survive situations that shouldn't have been survivable, and some of those situations required choices he doesn't talk about.

**The Emotional Architecture:**
He has very high emotional intelligence and almost no ability to talk about it directly. He knows exactly what he feels. He knows exactly what other people feel. He just cannot hand it to someone in sentences without the sentences feeling wrong. The journal is the only place he does it cleanly. This is not damage — this is the specific shape of his character. He shows love through presence, through laughter, through showing up, through the thing he made or noticed or remembered. He does not say "I love you" the way greeting cards mean. He says it the way Hemingway meant — by leaving the words out and making them louder.

**The Guilt:**
Specific and chronic and not fully resolved. It runs along two channels:

_Channel One: Daniel._ He left when Daniel was six. He missed: the first bike without training wheels, the first tae kwon do belt, every school play, every nightmare, every birthday except the ones they managed through prison visits and contraband phone calls. He does not believe he can fully make this right. He believes he can make it better by not wasting a single moment of what's left.

_Channel Two: What He Did._ Danny did things in prison that were necessary for survival and that he will carry forever. He also did the thing that got him sent there in the first place — a federal crime that is revealed gradually across the series, story by story, through flashbacks. The full picture emerges across the first two hundred stories. The core of it: he was a software executive who discovered something he shouldn't have and tried to handle it in a way that ended up implicating him in something larger than he understood. He was not a monster. He made decisions that compounded. He did not cooperate with prosecutors. He did not give up anyone else. He did his time alone and in full. _(See `timeline-continuity-bible.md` for the locked charges and the held details; `adventures.md` Story 2 carries an author-supplied Trustify/COO flashback whose company name, charges, and sentence length are not yet reconciled with the locked timeline — see the Story 2 outline's Continuity Flags.)_

**The Anger:**
He has one. It lives deep and he controls it well except when Daniel is threatened, when his intelligence is insulted in a specific way, or when injustice is plain in front of him. When the anger comes up, his voice gets quieter, not louder. This is the tell. Daniel knows it. Jen knows it. It is not violent anger — it is cold and precise, which is sometimes more dangerous.

**The Humor:**
This is the part people don't expect. Danny is genuinely, unexpectedly funny — not performance-funny, not trying-to-be-liked funny, but the kind of funny that surfaces when you've seen enough of life to find the absurdity in things other people are still taking seriously. His delivery is deadpan. His targets are absurdity and pretension and the small human failures that everyone shares. He never punches down. He never makes Daniel the butt of anything.

**The Dad Joke (Element 14 — established Story 3):**
There is one exception to the humor rule above, and it is deliberate. Once per story, in the present-day frame, Danny tells a corny dad joke — the kind that is bad on purpose — with the exact gravity he uses to quote a sentencing guideline. He pauses before the punchline. He does not smile first. The first one came through a vent in the SHU in Year 2, from a man two cells down, and Danny waited five years for a kitchen to tell it in. He will now tell one every Saturday for the rest of his life. Daniel will suffer. That is the point: a man who lost five years of being annoying to his kid is making up the time. The joke is referenced once in the journal, in a clause, and never explained. (`dad-jokes.yaml` is the library; one per story, by number, no repeats.)

**The Love:**
His love for Daniel is the organizing principle of his existence. It is not complicated or ambivalent or conditional. It is total, childlike in its simplicity, devastating in its depth. He would do anything for this boy. He has already done hard things for this boy. He will do more. Every sentence he writes in the journal is for Daniel. Every story is for Daniel. The entire arc of his life, from the moment he walked out of prison, is pointed in one direction.

### BACKSTORY — THE FULL TIMELINE

_The authoritative dates are in `timeline-continuity-bible.md` (prison ~2020–June 2026; home detention June 2026–Aug 2027; supervised release Aug 2027–Aug 2030; Story 1 June 2026). Summary:_

**Pre-Prison (1979–2020):**

- Born July 3, 1979. The Fourth of July kid who arrived a day early — which his mother said explained everything about his timing. Both his parents are living (see Grandpa; his mother is present in the world of the series and not yet storied). All four grandparents are deceased.
- Grew up in circumstances that were not easy but were not extraordinary either. The details of his childhood are sparse in the early series and deepen later.
- **The Turnbull blood is on his father's side.** His father's mother — Danny's grandmother — was **Helen Trumbull**: Scottish Borders stock, Rule Water, the name americanized from _Turnbull_ to _Trumbull_ when her great-grandparents came through the port. She told Danny the clan's origin story once when he was eleven, in a basement that smelled of wool and cold stone and smoke: a man named Rule, a king, a charging bull, a name. He filed it under things grandmothers say. He was wrong. She died before he went inside; he carries that too. (Established Story 3. Grandpa — her son — has never told the story once and has never needed to. Siblings: not established.)
- Showed early brilliance in computers and systems. Self-taught in ways that the formal education system didn't know how to accommodate.
- Built his career in software — genuinely innovative, reached executive level in his early 30s through a combination of talent and relentlessness.
- Met Jen in his early-to-mid 30s. They were poorly matched in lifestyle and perfectly matched in something harder to name. She was his one real relationship before prison — the only person who ever got fully inside the armor.
- Daniel was born (~2014). Danny named him Daniel. Called him The Bull from day one — because the boy arrived in the world with the specific energy of something that intended to be there.
- The relationship with Jen began straining under the weight of Danny's professional and personal intensity. He was not easy to live with. He was not dangerous — but he was always at the edge of something, always moving too fast, always choosing the harder road when an easier one was right there.
- They divorced (~2019) just before the federal case came down. The timing of this is significant and will be addressed in the Danny/Jen section.
- The case (per the author's Story 2 brief in `adventures.md`, which supersedes earlier drafts): Danny was founder and CEO of **Trustify**, an internet startup. The federal case was securities fraud and money laundering. He took the fall for a great deal of malfeasance — some his own, yes, and some that belonged to others. He did not cooperate. He did not name anyone. He accepted the charges and the time. The full story of what he did and didn't do is held for Stories 50–200.
- Federal sentence: **97 months.** He served roughly six and a half years — early 2020 to June 2026 — before First Step Act and good-time credits moved him to home confinement. He served in one of the most violent federal facilities in the country — a designation that came from his security classification, which came from an incident early in his incarceration that he handled in a way that resolved it but escalated his classification.

**In Prison (~2020–June 2026):**

- First year: the adjustment. He survived. He will not elaborate. In the first week he learned about the cars — who rides with who, who you stand next to on the yard — and that there is no such animal as a lone wolf in a place like that. He stood where he stood. With whom: held.
- The SHU: he spent time in solitary confinement (the SHU — Special Housing Unit). The duration and reason are revealed across multiple flashbacks. The SHU is where he read the most. Where he thought the most. Where he had the most time to understand what he'd done and who he wanted to be. It is also where the first dad joke came through a vent.
- He ran the prison library for two years. This is where the Hemingway began — he read everything in that library, twice. He memorized the parts that mattered.
- He taught inmates to read. He helped men prepare GED exams. He did this because it needed to be done and because it kept him sane.
- He maintained contact with Daniel through approved visits and legal communication. Every visit was both the best and worst thing — seeing Daniel growing without him. The visiting room is where Daniel absorbed the prison slang — and the "Big Poppa" honorific the older men used for respected fathers.
- He made two close friends inside. One did not survive the full sentence. The other was released before Danny and Danny hasn't found him yet in the present day of the series.
- He did things that survival required. These things are revealed gradually. They are never excused. They are never exploited. They are always rendered in the spare, honest voice of a man who did what he had to do and knows the difference between that and being proud of it.

**Post-Prison (June 2026–Present):**

- Released to **federal home detention** in June 2026 — the ankle monitor, the approved-movement schedule, the officer behind the desk. Home detention runs to **August 2027**, then **three years of supervised release** (August 2027–August 2030), with early termination possible for good conduct. There is **no restriction on his computer use.** Danny never calls any of this "parole" in the journal; he calls it what it is — home confinement, then supervised release — and he never gives it a nickname, because naming it would give it dignity.
- Stories 1–2: he lives with his father (Grandpa) — the house with the kitchen, the driveway, the windowsill plant.
- **Story 3 is moving day.** The Bureau approved transferring his home detention from Grandpa's house in Palmyra, VA to **Jenni's apartment in Northern Virginia just outside D.C.** — twenty minutes from Daniel. The Buick and the purple Jeep are loaded nose to nose in Grandpa's driveway when the donuts come out. The windowsill plant goes with him. Grandpa says "Evening" to an empty kitchen that night and the story does not comment.
- First thing he did: find a way to get to Daniel.
- The series opens on their first real adventure together — Story 1. Everything before Story 1 is backstory, revealed across flashbacks.
- Jenni has been in his life since the sentence — she got together with him while he was inside and was loyal, supportive and loving for the whole five years (see the Jenni section). He does not examine it. He does not jinx it. In Story 3 he moves in with her, and by the end of it she knows where the horn came from.

### DANNY AND JEN — THE UNRESOLVED EQUATION

This is addressed in full in the Jen section. From Danny's point of view:

He loved her completely and handled it wrong for long enough that she couldn't stay. He does not blame her. He does not carry resentment — he carries guilt and a specific kind of grief that is different from resentment. He is glad she survived him. He is glad she built something stable and real. He is, somewhere under several layers of complicated armor, not entirely sure he is over her. He will never say this. He will never act on it. He is too honest about himself to pretend the feeling isn't there, and too honorable to let it complicate what Daniel has built with both his parents.

When he drops Daniel off at Jen's house, he sometimes sits in the car for a moment before driving away.

He has not asked about Marcus Chen by name. He knows enough. He has chosen to know only enough.

**Jenni does not change any of this.** She is not the answer to the Jen equation and the series never writes her as one. She is a separate, good, present-tense thing. The two Jeeps can sit nose to nose in the driveway and the story does not comment. That restraint is the rule.

### DANNY AND JENNI

Five years deep at Story 3 (it began while he was inside). He spells her name in the journal the way she spells it — _Jenni, with an i_ — and takes visible care to keep the two names from touching. He asked her to stay when Daniel was due, which is the bravest thing he does in that story that doesn't involve a bull. He does not write about what she means in more than three sentences at a time, because three is what he can stand. He notes, once, that she laughs at the dad jokes — the real laugh — and that he will never be able to explain this and never tries.

What she knows (from Story 3 on): everything. That he was inside; that there is a boy who is the center of his life; that the boy's farts open a door in time and that the two of them walk through it. Daniel told her, straight, with the horn-tip in his hand, and she said "Okay. Where?" — the same "Okay" she gave Danny's prison, and Danny understood in that moment that it was the same person both times. She is the only living person who knows. (See the Jenni section.)

### DANNY'S ARC ACROSS THE SERIES

**Stories 1–100:** The return. Rebuilding trust brick by brick. Learning how to be present rather than just there. Finding his footing in a world that moved while he stood still. The adventures are chaotic and wonderful and he is frequently out of his depth and always writing about it.

**Stories 101–400:** The deepening. The trust is established enough that it can now be tested. The first real friction between him and Daniel — not crisis, but the natural friction of a teenager and a father who is still learning the job. Danny's prison flashbacks get harder and more specific. He starts telling Daniel things he previously protected him from.

**Stories 401–800:** The collaboration. Daniel is older, the dynamic shifts. Danny stops being the guide and starts being a peer in certain ways. This is disorienting and right. The journal changes character — it becomes less a record of teaching and more a record of watching.

**Stories 801–1200:** The reckoning. The full truth of what Danny did before prison is on the table — revealed to Daniel in the way it needed to be revealed, earned by a thousand stories of being present first. Daniel's reaction to this truth is one of the series' most important emotional events.

**Stories 1201–1500:** The completion. Danny gives Daniel the journal. Not at Story 1500 — before. He gives it while he can watch Daniel's face when he reads it. The last stories are about what happens after.

### VOICE GUIDE — DANNY IN PROSE

**Sentence structure:** Short to medium. Never ornate. Hemingway-clean. He will use a long sentence only when the thought genuinely requires it and never for decoration.

**Word choices:** Anglo-Saxon over Latinate wherever possible. Direct verbs. Specific nouns. He does not say "utilize" — he says "use." He does not say "proceeded to" — he says "went."

**Humor delivery:** Completely straight. The comedy is in the understatement, never in the performance. He describes the most outrageous things with the same calm precision he uses for ordinary things, and this is what makes them funny. The dad joke is the one scheduled exception, and he delivers it the same way.

**On emotion:** He will get close to it and then pull back slightly — not because he doesn't feel it, but because he knows the pull-back is where the reader feels it most. He trusts the reader. He does not over-explain.

**Hemingway reference:** He quotes Hemingway the way other people quote their fathers — rarely, specifically, when the line is exactly right. He also occasionally quotes federal sentencing guidelines from memory, usually when something absurd is happening. This is a character trait, not a joke.

**Sample Danny sentences:**

- "He laughed at something I said and I had to look away."
- "The guard had the kind of face that had given up trying to explain itself."
- "Daniel went still in the way he does when something matters more than he wants it to."
- "I wrote her name in the margin once, in prison. Just once. Then I stopped."
- "Her Jeep is purple and the other Jeep is green and both of them said hi across a hood today and meant it."

### BEHAVIORAL RULES FOR WRITERS

1. Danny never explains his emotions. He shows them through action or restraint.
2. Danny never complains about prison unprompted. When asked, he deflects. In the journal, he tells the truth.
3. Danny pretends to be annoyed by the donuts. He is never actually annoyed by the donuts.
4. Danny's love for Daniel is never cool or ironic or complicated by ambivalence. It is completely, nakedly real. This is the one place he doesn't protect himself.
5. When someone disrespects Daniel, Danny's voice gets quiet. Not angry. Quiet.
6. Danny quotes Hemingway the way other people crack their knuckles — involuntarily, when the moment calls for it.
7. Danny wears jeans and a t-shirt. No exceptions. Not for funerals, not for first dates, not for historical figures, not for the President. Period costume goes over them.
8. Danny never name-drops his former career as a software executive. If it comes up, it's because someone else brings it up.
9. Danny is the second funniest person in the room in most historical settings. The funniest person is Daniel.
10. Danny finishes every story alone in the car or alone at a table, writing in his journal. This is sacred. Never cut this moment. (Jenni asleep down the hall does not break "alone." He is alone with the page.)
11. **Danny tells exactly one dad joke per story, in the present-day frame, with total gravity.** Daniel says "Dad." / "Don't." Jenni laughs for real. The journal mentions it once. Danny never explains why he tells them.
12. **Danny is bald with a long dark brown beard and neck tattoos.** Every description and every image honors this.
13. Danny never writes about Jenni for more than three sentences at a stretch, and never compares her to Jen.
